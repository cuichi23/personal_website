Stumme Karte: a blind map of Europe

An interactive blind map of Europe. The outlines, rivers, mountain ridges, capital dots and landmark squares are all there; the names are not. Five of them arrive at a time as chips you drag to where they belong, and a name that lands correctly stays on the map. 170 names in German, English, Spanish and French, built from public-domain Natural Earth data on an equal-area projection.

Why this exists

My children are learning the geography of Europe, and the exercise their school uses is the old one: a stumme Karte, a blind map. The map keeps everything that is drawn — coastlines, borders, rivers, mountain ridges, a dot for every capital — and loses everything that is written. You get the names on a separate list and put them back where they belong.

It is a good exercise because it separates two things that a labelled map mixes up. Recognizing a shape is not the same as knowing where it is, and a map with the names on it lets you do neither: your eye reads the word and stops. Take the word away and the shape has to carry the work.

The paper version has two problems. It is single-use, and it needs someone to check it. This one has neither. It picks five names, it knows where each of them belongs, it says so immediately, and it remembers which ones you have already placed so the next five are more likely to be new ground. Nothing leaves the page: the progress lives in your own browser and the map is one file served from this site.

So geht es

Für die Kinder, auf Deutsch. Unten liegen fünf Namen. Zieh jeden Namen an die Stelle, an die er gehört, und lass ihn dort los. Auf einem Handy oder Tablet geht es auch so: erst den Namen antippen, dann die Karte. Wer lieber die Tastatur nimmt, wählt den Namen mit Tab und Enter, schiebt das rote Kreuz mit den Pfeiltasten (mit Shift geht es schneller) und legt den Namen mit Enter ab.

Richtig abgelegte Namen bleiben auf der Karte stehen. Die Karte füllt sich also, während du spielst — das ist der eigentliche Zweck. Oben wählst du das Thema (Länder, Hauptstädte, Meere, Flüsse, Gebirge, Inseln und Halbinseln, Sehenswürdigkeiten) und die Stufe: Leicht fragt nur die großen und bekannten Dinge, Mittel nimmt einiges dazu, Alles auch Montenegro, Rügen und den Guadalquivir. Mit DE · EN · ES · FR wechselst du die Sprache — der Karte und der Namen —, mit Zeig es mir gibst du bei einem Namen auf, und ganz rechts steht, wie viele der 170 Namen du schon einmal richtig hattest.

Zoomen und Vollbild. Oben rechts in der Karte sitzen vier Knöpfe: Vollbild, größer, kleiner, und — sobald die Karte vergrößert ist — zurück zur ganzen Karte. Am Rechner zoomst du auch mit Strg und dem Mausrad, auf dem Handy mit zwei Fingern. Ist die Karte vergrößert, schiebst du sie mit einem Finger (oder mit gedrückter Maustaste) herum; mit der Tastatur geht es mit +, -, 0 und, solange kein Name gewählt ist, mit den Pfeiltasten. Das Vollbild lohnt sich auf einem kleinen Bildschirm, und für Luxemburg oder Montenegro lohnt sich das Zoomen immer.

Zwei Regeln, die die Karte streng nimmt, weil sie genau das sind, was man lernen soll: ein Meer liegt im Wasser, also zählt ein Treffer auf Sizilien nicht als Mittelmeer, und eine Insel liegt an Land, also zählt ein Treffer neben Kreta nicht als Kreta.

What is on the map

Nothing on the map is labelled, so every symbol has to be readable on its own.

Symbol What it is
cream outline a country the quiz asks for
grey outline a country outside Europe, drawn for context only
orange dot a capital city
dark blue square a landmark
thin blue line a river
brown line a mountain ridge

The 170 names behind those symbols are 42 countries, 42 capitals, 26 rivers, 18 seas and other waters, 16 islands and peninsulas, 16 landmarks and 10 mountain ranges. Each one carries a level from one to three, which is a guess at how early a child meets it rather than a measure of anything. A country and its capital are linked both ways, so placing Oslo tells you it is the capital of Norway and placing Norway tells you its capital is Oslo.

Every name exists four times: German, English, Spanish and French. The two added later are not a machine translation of the first two. They are a table in the generator, keyed by the item's id and kept out of the geometry tuples, because widening 170 positional tuples by two columns is the kind of edit that silently shifts a latitude. The country-and-capital note is assembled rather than listed, which turned out to need a little grammar: capital of the United Kingdom is capital del Reino Unido and capitale du Royaume-Uni, while Spain takes a bare de in both. So the generator carries a second, small table of the genitive form of each country name in those two languages, and the note is built from it.

The names can be refreshed without the map. tools/build_europe_map.py --relabel applies the name tables to an existing map.json and refuses to write if a single coordinate changed. That matters because the geometry needs three public-domain input files that are not in this repository, and correcting one spelling should not mean downloading 50 MB of coastlines first.

How it is built

The geometry comes from Natural Earth, which is in the public domain, by way of the 1:50 m countries, rivers and lakes files. tools/build_europe_map.py in this site's repository projects them and writes the one file the applet loads.

The projection is Albers equal-area conic, with standard parallels at 43° N and 62° N. Equal-area is the point: a child comparing Sweden with Spain should be comparing the real thing, and the Mercator projection every web map defaults to would make Scandinavia roughly twice the size it has any right to be. The price is that shapes are slightly sheared at the edges of the window, which is the trade every map makes in one direction or the other.

A few decisions worth naming, because they are visible:

  • The window runs from about 26° W to 46° E. That reaches the Volga and the Caucasus but stops short of the Urals, so the Urals are not among the mountain ranges the quiz asks for. Putting them in would have meant shrinking everything west of Berlin.
  • Rings are cut in latitude and longitude before they are projected. Russia's outline runs all the way to the dateline; projected first and cut afterwards, it folds back across the map as one polygon covering most of Europe. Cutting first, after unwrapping the longitudes across the antimeridian, leaves the European end of it.
  • Hit testing differs by what is being asked for. A country is hit by landing inside its own outline, which the browser answers exactly through isPointInFill. A river or a mountain ridge is hit by landing within a tolerance of its line. A sea, an island or a landmark is a point with a radius, and for seas and islands the drop also has to be on the right side of the coastline. Countries smaller than a certain area get a small tolerance around their label point as well, because Luxembourg is about three pixels wide on a phone.
  • The zoom is the viewBox and nothing else. Dragging, tapping and the arrow-key crosshair all turn a screen position into a map position through getScreenCTM(), which already carries the viewBox, the element's CSS size and any page scaling. Moving the viewBox therefore moves the hit test with it, and no part of the game had to learn that a zoom exists. To hold a point still while zooming about it, the applet projects the same screen point before and after the change and shifts the view by the difference, which needs no assumption about how the map is laid out — and so keeps working in full screen, where the map is letterboxed.
  • Marks are measured in screen pixels, geography in map units. A capital dot drawn at three map units would be two pixels across on a laptop, four on a phone and sixteen once you zoom in. So the applet writes the current map-units-per-pixel into a CSS variable and every dot, label and line thickness is a multiple of it. They come out the same size on screen at any width and any zoom, while the coastlines underneath stay geography. This replaced a width-based media query that had guessed at the same thing.
  • The mountain ridges are drawn by hand, as a handful of points each along the spine of the range. Natural Earth has no usable range geometry at this scale. They are right to within a map's width of line and no better, which is enough to point at a range and not enough to trace one.
  • Capitals, landmarks, seas and peninsulas are hand-entered coordinates. That is the part a dataset cannot supply and the part most likely to carry a mistake. If something sits in the wrong place, it is mine and I would like to hear about it.

At 1:50 m several rivers a German child is expected to know simply are not in the data — Weser, Main, Meuse, Tiber, Shannon, Guadalquivir, Neman — so the map does not ask for them. The 1:10 m files would carry them at roughly ten times the size. That is the next thing I would change.

What it does not do

It does not teach anything by itself. It asks and it checks, which is a smaller job, and it is honest about the difference. There is no account, no timer, no streak and no badge: the only number it keeps is how many of the 170 names you have placed correctly at least once, and a button to throw that away again.

Full screen comes in two flavours because one of them is not always available. Where the Fullscreen API works, the browser supplies the backdrop; where it refuses — an iPhone, mostly, which grants full screen to video and to little else — the applet pins itself over the page instead. The two end up looking the same, which is the point, but only the first one hides the browser's own chrome.
