Analog circuits · Chapter 3

Capacitors and the RC circuit

The first component with memory, and therefore the first differential equation. Step response and the time constant, the complex impedance that turns calculus back into algebra, the low-pass and the high-pass as one circuit read from two nodes, and why a chain of filters is not the product of its filters.

Chapter 3 of 8 · 22 November 2026

A component that remembers

Everything in the first two chapters obeyed a relation between the voltage across a component and the current through it at the same instant. A resistor has no memory. Tell it the voltage now and it tells you the current now, and nothing about the past enters.

A capacitor stores charge, and charge is an integral of current. That one fact is the whole chapter.

$$\begin{aligned} q &= C\,v ,\\[2pt] i &= \frac{\mathrm{d}q}{\mathrm{d}t} = C\,\frac{\mathrm{d}v}{\mathrm{d}t} , \end{aligned}$$

where $q$ is the stored charge, $v$ the voltage across the plates, $i$ the current into the positive plate, and $C$ the capacitance in farads. For a parallel-plate capacitor $C = \varepsilon_0\varepsilon_\mathrm{r}A/d$, with $A$ the plate area and $d$ their separation, which is why a 1 F capacitor used to be an absurd object and is now a thumbnail.

Three consequences follow immediately, and all three get used constantly.

  • Current flows only while the voltage changes. Hold $v$ constant and $i$ is zero. At DC a capacitor is an open circuit.
  • The voltage cannot jump. A discontinuity in $v$ would need an infinite current. Over an interval short compared with everything else, a charged capacitor behaves like a voltage source holding its present value.
  • It stores energy rather than dissipating it. Integrating $p = vi$ gives $W = \tfrac{1}{2}Cv^2$, and that energy comes back out. A resistor's energy does not.

The second point is the dual of something you already know. A resistor set by Ohm's law has no state; a capacitor has exactly one number of state, its voltage. Everything a capacitor does to a circuit is a consequence of the circuit having to charge or discharge that one number.

The first differential equation

Put a resistor in series with a capacitor and drive the pair from a voltage source $V$. Kirchhoff's voltage law gives $v_R + v_C = V$, Ohm's law gives $v_R = R\,i$, and the capacitor gives $i = C\,\dot v_C$. Substituting,

$$RC\,\frac{\mathrm{d}v_C}{\mathrm{d}t} + v_C = V .$$

First order, linear, constant coefficients. With an initial voltage $v_C(0) = V_0$ the solution is

$$\begin{aligned} v_C(t) &= V + (V_0 - V)\,\mathrm{e}^{-t/\tau} ,\\[2pt] \tau &= RC . \end{aligned}$$

The capacitor voltage leaves where it was and approaches where the source wants it, exponentially, with one time scale. Check that $\tau$ really is a time: ohm times farad is $(\mathrm{V/A})\cdot(\mathrm{C/V}) = \mathrm{C/A} = \mathrm{s}$.

The current is what the resistor sees across it,

$$i(t) = \frac{V - v_C(t)}{R} = \frac{V - V_0}{R}\,\mathrm{e}^{-t/\tau} .$$

The current jumps and the voltage does not. At $t = 0^+$ the capacitor still holds $V_0$, so the full difference appears across the resistor and the current is at its maximum. That is the standard picture and it is worth having in mind before looking at any RC waveform.

The numbers people actually quote:

after fraction of the way there
$1\tau$ 63.2 %
$2\tau$ 86.5 %
$3\tau$ 95.0 %
$5\tau$ 99.3 %

"Five time constants" is the engineering definition of settled, and it is a choice, not a theorem. The exponential never arrives.

The bench

A square wave is a step up followed by a step down, so one picture shows charging, discharging and the thing that matters most: what happens when the drive changes faster than the circuit can follow.

Drive the filter slowly and the output copies the input with rounded corners. Speed the drive up past the cutoff and the exponentials no longer have time to curve. What is left is a triangle, and a triangle is the integral of a square wave. The RC low-pass becomes an integrator when you run it far above its cutoff, and it pays for that with almost all of the amplitude. Chapter 6 buys the integration back without the price.

The move that makes everything easier

Solving a differential equation for every input would be unbearable. There is a standard way out, and it is the same one used for normal modes in mechanics.

The circuit is linear and time invariant, so a complex exponential is an eigenfunction of it: put $\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega t}$ in and you get $\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega t}$ out, at the same frequency, scaled by a complex number. Write every quantity as

$$\begin{aligned} v(t) &= \mathrm{Re}\left\{\underline{V}\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega t}\right\} ,\\[2pt] i(t) &= \mathrm{Re}\left\{\underline{I}\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega t}\right\} , \end{aligned}$$

where the underlined quantities are complex amplitudes, carrying magnitude and phase. Differentiation becomes multiplication by $\mathrm{i}\omega$. Putting this into $i = C\dot v$ gives $\underline{I} = \mathrm{i}\omega C\,\underline{V}$, and therefore

$$Z_C = \frac{\underline{V}}{\underline{I}} = \frac{1}{\mathrm{i}\omega C} = \frac{-\mathrm{i}}{\omega C} .$$

This is the impedance of a capacitor: an Ohm's law for sinusoids, complex because the response is not in phase with the drive. Its magnitude is $1/(\omega C)$ and its phase is $-90^\circ$, which says the current leads the voltage by a quarter cycle. The capacitor is already pushing charge in while the voltage is still low.

The two limits are worth saying in words:

$$\begin{aligned} \omega \to 0: \quad & |Z_C| \to \infty \quad\text{(an open circuit at DC)},\\ \omega \to \infty: \quad & |Z_C| \to 0 \quad\text{(a short circuit at high frequency)}. \end{aligned}$$

Those two sentences are most of what a decoupling capacitor does for a living: it is invisible to the supply rail and a short to anything that wiggles.

The payoff is that chapter 2 now applies unchanged. Series impedances add, parallel impedances combine by the product-over-sum rule, the voltage divider is still the voltage divider, Thévenin and Norton still hold. Replace $R$ by $Z$ and every result carries over. That is the reason impedance is introduced at all: it turns calculus back into the algebra you already did.

Two filters, one circuit

Take a resistor and a capacitor in series across a source, and read the output from one node or the other. Nothing else changes.

$$\begin{aligned} H_\mathrm{LP}(\omega) &= \frac{Z_C}{R + Z_C} = \frac{1}{1 + \mathrm{i}\omega RC} ,\\[4pt] H_\mathrm{HP}(\omega) &= \frac{R}{R + Z_C} = \frac{\mathrm{i}\omega RC}{1 + \mathrm{i}\omega RC} . \end{aligned}$$

Across the capacitor you get a low-pass; across the resistor a high-pass. They are the same divider read from two places, so they add to one at every frequency, which is a useful check and an occasionally useful circuit.

Both have the same corner, where the resistance and the reactance are equal in magnitude:

$$\omega_\mathrm{c} = \frac{1}{RC} , \qquad f_\mathrm{c} = \frac{\omega_\mathrm{c}}{2\pi} .$$

At that frequency $|H| = 1/\sqrt{2} = 0.707$, the delivered power is halved, and the phase has moved by $45^\circ$. In decibels $20\log_{10}(1/\sqrt 2) = -3.01$, which is where the name −3 dB point comes from. It marks a factor of two in power, not a factor of two in amplitude, and conflating the two is the most common unit error in the subject.

One number to carry around: 1 kΩ with 1 µF gives $\tau = 1\ \mathrm{ms}$ and $f_\mathrm{c} = 159\ \mathrm{Hz}$. Everything else scales from there, since

$$f_\mathrm{c}\,[\mathrm{Hz}] = \frac{159\,155}{R\,[\Omega]\;C\,[\mu\mathrm{F}]} .$$

Reading it as a Bode plot

Plot $20\log_{10}|H|$ against $\log\omega$ and the low-pass becomes two straight lines: flat at 0 dB below the corner, falling at 20 dB per decade above it. The asymptotes meet exactly at $\omega_\mathrm{c}$, and the true curve sits 3 dB below that meeting point.

The phase is

$$\arg H_\mathrm{LP} = -\arctan(\omega RC) ,$$

which is $0^\circ$ at DC, $-45^\circ$ at the corner and $-90^\circ$ far above it. The transition is slow: $-5.7^\circ$ at a tenth of the corner and $-84.3^\circ$ at ten times it. A single pole costs 20 dB per decade and $90^\circ$, and the phase is already moving a decade before the magnitude notices. That sentence is the reason chapter 6 has a stability section.

The same information, twice

The step response and the frequency response are not two properties of the circuit. They are one property written in two coordinate systems, and the dictionary between them is

$$\tau = \frac{1}{\omega_\mathrm{c}} = \frac{1}{2\pi f_\mathrm{c}} .$$

The most used consequence: the 10 % to 90 % rise time of a first-order step is $t_\mathrm{r} = \tau\ln 9 = 2.197\,\tau$, so

$$t_\mathrm{r}\,f_\mathrm{c} = \frac{\ln 9}{2\pi} \approx 0.35 .$$

A scope with 100 MHz of bandwidth cannot show an edge faster than about 3.5 ns, and a 3.5 ns edge on its screen tells you nothing except that the scope is a first-order system. Anything faster than that is the instrument, not the signal.

Cascading is not multiplying

Put one RC low-pass after another and the obvious guess is that the transfer functions multiply. They do not, because the second stage loads the first. This is chapter 2's problem wearing a frequency-dependent coat.

Analysed properly, two stages with $R_1, C_1$ then $R_2, C_2$ give

$$H(s) = \frac{1}{1 + s\,(R_1C_1 + R_2C_2 + R_1C_2) + s^2 R_1C_1R_2C_2} ,$$

with $s = \mathrm{i}\omega$, and the term $R_1C_2$ is the loading. With two identical stages the exact answer and the naive one are

$$\begin{aligned} \text{loaded:}\quad & \frac{1}{1 + 3\,sRC + (sRC)^2} ,\\[4pt] \text{naive product:}\quad & \frac{1}{1 + 2\,sRC + (sRC)^2} . \end{aligned}$$

Same two poles' worth of structure, different damping. The real cascade is slower and less peaked than the product predicts, and the discrepancy does not go away by making the components nicer.

Two fixes, both already familiar:

  1. Scale the stages. Make $R_2 \gg R_1$ and $C_2 \ll C_1$ so that $R_1C_2$ is negligible. The rule of ten from chapter 2, applied to a product of two quantities instead of a ratio.
  2. Put a buffer between them. A device with a high input impedance and a low output impedance makes the product exact. That device is the subject of chapter 6, and this is the second time it has been needed.

Where this bites

  • Your probe is part of the circuit. A scope probe presents roughly 10 pF. At a node with 100 kΩ of source resistance that is a corner at 159 kHz. You are measuring the probe as much as the signal, which is why a 10× probe trades amplitude for a tenth of the capacitance.
  • Decoupling. Every supply pin gets a capacitor to ground because the supply wiring has inductance and resistance, and the chip's current demand is not constant. The capacitor is a local source with a very small internal resistance, in exactly chapter 1's sense.
  • Noise sets a floor you cannot resistor your way out of. A resistor and a capacitor alone, in thermal equilibrium, put a mean-square voltage on the capacitor of
$$\overline{v^2} = \frac{k_\mathrm{B}T}{C} ,$$

independent of $R$. Equipartition: one degree of freedom, $\tfrac12 C \overline{v^2} = \tfrac12 k_\mathrm{B}T$. At 300 K on 1 pF that is 64 µV rms. Making the resistor quieter does not help, because a larger resistor makes more noise over a narrower band and the two cancel exactly. Only more capacitance helps.

Exercises

  1. Show that the high-pass has the same time constant $RC$, and find its response to a step of height $V$. (It jumps to $V$ and decays to zero: the capacitor blocks the DC the step leaves behind.)
  2. A 10× scope probe is a 9 MΩ resistor in parallel with a trimmer capacitor, feeding the scope's 1 MΩ and 20 pF. What must the trimmer be set to so that the division ratio is the same at all frequencies? This is what the little screw on a probe adjusts.
  3. Derive $\overline{v^2} = k_\mathrm{B}T/C$ the long way: integrate the Johnson noise density $4k_\mathrm{B}TR$ through $|H_\mathrm{LP}|^2$ over all frequencies, and watch $R$ cancel.
  4. Two identical RC stages are cascaded. Using the exact expression above, by what factor is the overall −3 dB frequency lower than that of one stage? Compare with the answer the naive product gives, and check both on bench 6 by matching the single-stage curve.
