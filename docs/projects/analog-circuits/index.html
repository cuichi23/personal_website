Analog circuits in theory and application

A course in analog circuit design for people who already have the mathematics and the physics: sources and internal resistance, resistive networks, RC and RLC, diodes, operational amplifiers, the motifs built from them, and transistors. Every chapter carries interactive benches rather than worked numbers. Eight chapters, twelve benches, under construction.

Under construction. All eight chapters are written and every bench works. What is still missing is the part only a bench can supply: measured numbers next to the predicted ones. As I build these circuits I will add what they actually did, and correct whatever the hardware disagrees with. Treat the chapters as derivations that have not yet been checked against a soldering iron.

Why this exists

I am a theoretical physicist, and I work on circuits that compute. At anabrid that means analog computers. In my own research it means networks of coupled oscillators built out of phase-locked loops. I think about them at the level of their dynamics. An oscillator is a phase, a coupling is a function of phase differences, a delay is a number. That level answers the questions I ask of it, and every result I have rests on it. It also stops one step short of the board. In my equations a phase-locked loop is a symbol. On a board it is a mixer, a loop filter with a real cutoff, an oscillator with a real tuning slope, and a few impedances I never wrote down. That step is the part a physicist is usually never taught properly: how a real circuit behaves when it is loaded, when its source is not ideal, when a signal has to get from one block to the next without being eaten on the way. So far I have taken that layer on trust. I would rather build these things than only model them.

That part is not hard. It is a small set of ideas applied over and over. This is my attempt to write it down the way I would want to have read it: derive the expression, then make the expression move, then say what it means when a component value goes to zero or to infinity, which is where every idealization in circuit theory dies.

Who it is for. Someone with first-year calculus and first-year physics: comfortable with a differential equation, with complex numbers, and with the idea of an impedance even if the word is new. No prior electronics is assumed. If you can read $v(t) = V_0\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega t}$ without flinching, the chapters will work.

What it is not. Not a replacement for a lab bench, and not a SPICE manual. Every bench on these pages evaluates one closed-form expression that the text derives. That is deliberate: a simulator hides which term dominates, and the point of a first course is to learn which term dominates.

How to use a chapter

Each chapter has the same three layers.

  1. The model. The governing relation, with every symbol named on the line after it.
  2. The bench. An interactive panel for that relation. The sliders run over decades, because component values do, and most of them reach a true zero and a true infinity at the ends. Go to the ends. The interesting failures live there.
  3. The limits. What the expression says when something is shorted, opened, matched or mismatched, and which of those a real component survives.

The benches need JavaScript, and nothing else: no account, no network traffic, no data leaving the page. Where a bench is unavailable, the surrounding text still carries the result as a formula.

The chapters

  1. 1 Sources and internal resistance ready

    The two idealizations and why they are duals of each other: a voltage source prescribes the voltage and lets the current follow, a current source does the opposite. Internal resistance as the single parameter that turns an idealization into a component. Which sources may be wired in series and which in parallel, and what the combination behaves as. Thévenin and Norton as two views of the same box.

  2. 2 Resistive networks and loading ready

    Kirchhoff's two laws, series and parallel, and the voltage divider: the circuit that appears inside every other circuit. Why the divider you build never has the ratio you designed, what output resistance means, where the rule of ten comes from, and why matching a load for maximum power throws half the power away.

  3. 3 Capacitors and the RC circuit ready

    Charge, the $i = C\,\mathrm{d}v/\mathrm{d}t$ relation, and the first differential equation of the course. Step response and the time constant; sinusoidal steady state and the complex impedance; the low-pass and the high-pass as the same two components read from two nodes. Bode magnitude and phase, the −3 dB point, and why a 20 dB/decade slope is a statement about a single pole.

  4. 4 Inductors, RLC and resonance ready

    The dual of the capacitor, the second-order equation, and the three damping regimes. Resonance, the quality factor, bandwidth, and the connection between a pole pair in the complex plane and a ringing step response. This is the chapter the oscillator work later builds on.

  5. 5 Diodes and the first nonlinearity ready

    The exponential characteristic, the thermal voltage, and what a load line does when the device is not a straight line. Rectifiers, clamps, limiters, and the small-signal resistance: the move of linearizing around an operating point, which is the move the whole rest of the course depends on.

  6. 6 Operational amplifiers ready

    The ideal op-amp as two rules and what they are worth. Inverting and non-inverting stages, the buffer and why a divider that could not drive anything suddenly can, summing and difference amplifiers, integrator and differentiator. Then the honest version: finite gain, finite bandwidth, the gain-bandwidth product, slew rate, input offset, and the stability question that decides whether a circuit works or sings.

  7. 7 Motifs: the blocks circuits are made of ready

    Comparator and Schmitt trigger, active filters, the instrumentation amplifier, current sources made from what chapter 6 provides, precision rectifiers, and the relaxation oscillator. The emphasis is on recognizing a motif inside a schematic you have never seen before.

  8. 8 Transistors ready

    The bipolar transistor and the MOSFET as controlled sources, biasing and the operating point, the small-signal model, and the three single-stage amplifiers. Then the transistor as a switch, the differential pair, the current mirror, and enough of a look inside an op-amp that chapter 6 stops being a black box.

Conventions

Fixed once, used everywhere.
Current direction Conventional current, from the positive terminal through the external circuit.
Passive sign convention For a resistor, current enters at the terminal marked positive. A positive power then means the component absorbs.
Lower case, upper case $v$, $i$ are instantaneous values; $V$, $I$ are constant or RMS values; $\hat V$ is an amplitude; $\underline{V}$ is a complex phasor.
Node voltages Always with respect to a named reference node, drawn as ground. There is no absolute voltage anywhere in this course.
Component values Engineering notation with SI prefixes: 4.7 kΩ, 100 nF, 1 µA.

Sources and further reading

The physics here is standard and nothing in it is mine. Where I follow a particular treatment I say so in the chapter. The books I keep going back to: Horowitz and Hill, The Art of Electronics (3rd edition) for the engineering judgement; Tietze and Schenk, Halbleiter-Schaltungstechnik for the German reference treatment; Sedra and Smith, Microelectronic Circuits for the device chapters; and for the analog-computing side, Ulmann, Analog Computing.

Corrections are welcome. If a derivation here is wrong, I would rather hear it than keep it.
