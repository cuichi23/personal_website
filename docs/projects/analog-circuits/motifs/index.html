Analog circuits · Chapter 7

Motifs: the blocks circuits are made of

A schematic is not a hundred components, it is eight or ten recognizable blocks. Positive feedback and hysteresis, the relaxation oscillator that makes its own time, active filters without an inductor, the instrumentation amplifier, current sources, precision rectifiers, and the four motifs an analog computer is assembled from.

Chapter 7 of 8 · 22 November 2026

Schematics are read, not solved

A page of analog circuitry looks like a hundred components. It is not. It is eight or ten blocks, each of which has a name, a transfer function and a characteristic shape on the page, wired in a sequence. Somebody fluent reads them the way you read words rather than letters, and the only way to get there is to learn the vocabulary.

This chapter is the vocabulary. Every block in it is built from chapters 1 to 6 and nothing else, and the useful skill is recognizing one inside a circuit you have never seen.

Positive feedback: the comparator and the Schmitt trigger

Everything in chapter 6 assumed the feedback went to the inverting input. Send it to the non-inverting input instead and the sign in $G = A/(1 - A\beta)$ flips. The loop gain is now positive, the denominator passes through zero, and the circuit has no stable operating point in its linear range. It snaps to one rail or the other and stays there.

That is not a failure, it is a decision-making circuit.

A comparator with no feedback at all outputs high when $v_+ > v_-$ and low otherwise. Useful, and badly behaved on a slow or noisy input: near the threshold the noise crosses back and forth and the output chatters.

A Schmitt trigger adds positive feedback through a divider, so the threshold moves away from wherever the input just came from. With a divider $R_1$ from the inverting input's reference and $R_2$ from the output, and rails at $\pm V_\mathrm{s}$, the two thresholds are

$$V_{\mathrm{th}\pm} = \pm\,\frac{R_1}{R_1 + R_2}\,V_\mathrm{s} ,$$

and the gap between them is the hysteresis window. An input must cross the whole window to flip the output back, so any noise smaller than the window is ignored. The cost is that the switching point now depends on which way the input is moving, so the circuit has memory. Hysteresis and memory are the same thing.

The relaxation oscillator

Take the Schmitt trigger and let its own output charge a capacitor back into its input through a resistor. The output drives the capacitor towards one rail; the capacitor reaches the threshold; the output flips; now it is driven the other way. Nothing settles, and the circuit produces a square wave and a triangle-ish ramp forever.

With $\beta = R_1/(R_1+R_2)$ the half period follows from chapter 3's exponential, started at $-\beta V_\mathrm{s}$ and aimed at $+V_\mathrm{s}$, stopped at $+\beta V_\mathrm{s}$:

$$\begin{aligned} T &= 2RC\,\ln\!\left(\frac{1+\beta}{1-\beta}\right)\\[4pt] &= 2RC\,\ln\!\left(1 + \frac{2R_1}{R_2}\right) . \end{aligned}$$

This is the first circuit in the course that makes its own time. Everything before it responded to something; this one has no input. It is worth being precise about why it can: the positive feedback removes the stable operating point, and the capacitor supplies the state that carries the circuit from one switch to the next. A relaxation oscillator and a resonant oscillator (chapter 4) are the two ways to do that, and they fail differently. The resonant one has good frequency stability because the frequency is set by $\sqrt{LC}$ and the amplitude by a nonlinearity. The relaxation one has terrible frequency stability, because the frequency is set by rails and thresholds that drift, and excellent amplitude stability, because the amplitude is the rails.

Active filters

Chapter 4 got a resonant response out of an inductor, and then explained why nobody wants an inductor. An op-amp provides the way out: feedback can produce a complex pole pair from resistors and capacitors alone.

The standard block is the Sallen-Key low-pass: two resistors, two capacitors and a buffer, with the first capacitor returned to the buffer's output instead of to ground. That one connection is what creates the second-order behaviour. For the equal-component version with gain $K$,

$$\omega_0 = \frac{1}{RC} , \qquad Q = \frac{1}{3 - K} .$$

Read that $Q$ expression carefully, because it contains the whole story of active filters. At $K = 1$ the $Q$ is $1/2$, safely overdamped. At $K = 3$ the $Q$ is infinite and the circuit oscillates. The pole pair is positioned by how much positive feedback is let through, and the distance to instability is a resistor ratio. The Butterworth response from chapter 4 needs $Q = 1/\sqrt2$, that is $K = 1.586$, and a 5 % error in that gain is a visible error in the passband.

Higher orders are made by cascading second-order sections, each with its own $\omega_0$ and $Q$ taken from a table, with buffers between them so that chapter 3's cascading problem does not return.

The same trick generalizes. A gyrator uses an op-amp to make a capacitor look like an inductor from one port, with an effective $L = R_1R_2C$. There is no coil, no stray field and no saturation, and the synthetic inductor has one end committed to ground. Below a few megahertz this is how resonance is built.

The instrumentation amplifier

Chapter 6's difference amplifier had two defects: its input resistance is low and unequal on the two inputs, and its common-mode rejection is set by resistor matching rather than by the amplifier.

The instrumentation amplifier fixes both with two more op-amps. Each input goes to its own non-inverting buffer stage, and the two stages share a single gain resistor $R_\mathrm{G}$ between their inverting inputs. A difference amplifier follows at unity gain. The result is

$$v_\mathrm{out} = \left(1 + \frac{2R}{R_\mathrm{G}}\right)(v_2 - v_1) ,$$

with both inputs now presenting the op-amp's own input resistance, and the gain set by one component. The subtlety is the good part: the input stage has a gain of one for the common mode and a gain of $1 + 2R/R_\mathrm{G}$ for the difference, because the common mode puts no voltage across $R_\mathrm{G}$ and therefore drives no current through it. The difference is amplified before the matched resistors see it, so their mismatch matters less by exactly the gain.

This is the front end of essentially every sensor measurement: strain gauges, thermocouples, electrode pairs, current-sense shunts. Anywhere the wanted signal is a small difference riding on a large common voltage.

Making a current source

Chapter 1 asked for a current source and never built one. The ingredients are now available, and the recipe is the same one as always: use feedback to hold something constant.

Current through a floating load. Put the load in the feedback path of an inverting stage. The virtual ground forces the input current to $v_\mathrm{in}/R$, and rule 2 sends all of it through the load, whatever the load is:

$$i_\mathrm{L} = -\frac{v_\mathrm{in}}{R} .$$

The load's resistance has dropped out, which is the definition of a current source. The compliance is the op-amp's output swing, exactly as chapter 1 predicted it would be.

Current through a grounded load. Harder, because the load cannot sit in the feedback path. The Howland current source uses a bridge of four resistors around one op-amp; it works, and its output resistance depends on the resistor matching in the same way a difference amplifier's rejection does. The usual practical answer instead puts a transistor in the loop, which is chapter 8.

Precision rectifier

A diode cannot rectify a 50 mV signal: the 0.7 V drop from chapter 5 swallows it. Put the diode inside an op-amp's feedback loop and the drop is divided by the loop gain, like every other error in chapter 6. The amplifier simply drives its output 0.7 V higher than it otherwise would, and the node that matters is where the feedback is taken from.

$$\text{effective drop} \approx \frac{V_\mathrm{f}}{1 + A\beta} .$$

With $A\beta = 10^4$ the 0.7 V has become 70 µV. The circuit is sometimes called a superdiode, and the technique is general: anything inside the loop is divided by the loop gain, so put the component you cannot fix where the feedback can see it. The cost is that when the diode turns off the loop is broken and the amplifier saturates, so the recovery is slow; the two-diode version keeps the loop closed in both halves and is what actually gets built.

The analog computer, in four motifs

Everything above is general electronics. Four of these blocks, specifically, are what a programmable analog computer is made of, and they are the reason this series exists.

motif what it does built from
integrator $v_\mathrm{out} = -\frac{1}{RC}\int v\,\mathrm{d}t$, with a settable initial condition and a reset chapter 6
summer weighted addition at a virtual ground, inputs not interacting chapter 6
coefficient multiplication by a constant in $[-1, 1]$, set by a potentiometer or a multiplying DAC chapter 2
multiplier the product of two signals chapter 5's logarithm, or chapter 8's differential pair

A differential equation is patched by solving for the highest derivative, feeding it to a chain of integrators, and closing the loops with summers and coefficients. The machine then solves it in continuous time, in parallel, with no time step and no numerical stability condition, because it is not approximating the equation — it is obeying a different physical system with the same equation.

What it costs is exactly the content of this course. The coefficient is only as good as the divider (chapter 2). The integrator drifts and has a finite loop gain (chapter 6). Every block loads the next one, so each is buffered, and the buffers have bandwidth (chapters 3 and 6). The multiplier's accuracy comes from how well two junctions match (chapters 5 and 8). The accuracy of an analog computer is a statement about its loading, its offsets and its matching, and there is no other place for it to come from.

Exercises

  1. A Schmitt trigger runs on $\pm12\ \mathrm{V}$ with $R_1 = 10\ \mathrm{k}\Omega$ and $R_2 = 100\ \mathrm{k}\Omega$. Find the thresholds and the window. Then add $R = 47\ \mathrm{k}\Omega$ and $C = 10\ \mathrm{nF}$ and find the oscillation frequency. Check both on bench 11.
  2. Show that an equal-component Sallen-Key section reaches $Q = 1/\sqrt{2}$ at $K = 1.586$, and find how far $K$ may drift before $Q$ changes by 10 %. This is why the gain-setting resistors in an active filter are the 1 % ones.
  3. An instrumentation amplifier is set to a gain of 100. Its output difference amplifier uses 1 % resistors. Estimate the common-mode rejection, and compare with the same 1 % resistors used in a plain difference amplifier at a gain of 100.
  4. Sketch the patch diagram for $\ddot x + 2\zeta\omega_0\dot x + \omega_0^2 x = 0$ using two integrators, one summer and two coefficients. Where does the initial condition for $\dot x$ enter, and what sets the time scale?
