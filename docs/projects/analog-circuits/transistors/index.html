Transistors

The controlled source that everything else is made of. The bipolar transistor and the MOSFET, biasing and the operating point, the small-signal model, the three single-stage amplifiers, and then the differential pair, the current mirror and enough of an op-amp's insides that chapter 6 stops being a black box.

Chapter 8 of 8 · 22 November 2026

One idea, two devices

A transistor is a controlled source. A small quantity at one terminal pair controls a large current at another, and the energy for that current comes from the supply rather than from the input. Everything else is detail about which quantity controls what and how the control curve is shaped.

Two families matter. Both are three-terminal devices and both are described by a characteristic that saturates into a near-horizontal line, which is chapter 1's current source appearing as a physical object.

bipolar (BJT) MOSFET
terminals base, emitter, collector gate, source, drain
controlled by $v_\mathrm{BE}$, exponentially $v_\mathrm{GS}$, above a threshold
input current $i_\mathrm{B} = i_\mathrm{C}/\beta$, nanoamps to microamps essentially zero
transconductance $g_\mathrm{m} = I_\mathrm{C}/V_\mathrm{T}$ $g_\mathrm{m} = \sqrt{2k I_\mathrm{D}}$
matching at a given current excellent needs area
as a switch a saturation voltage of ~0.2 V a resistance $R_\mathrm{DS(on)}$

The bipolar transistor

In the forward active region the collector current is

$$i_\mathrm{C} = I_\mathrm{S}\,\mathrm{e}^{v_\mathrm{BE}/V_\mathrm{T}} ,$$

which is the same exponential as chapter 5's diode, with the same $V_\mathrm{T} = k_\mathrm{B}T/q = 25.9\ \mathrm{mV}$ and the same 60 mV per decade. That is not an analogy: the base-emitter junction is a diode, and the collector collects the carriers that cross it.

The base current is $i_\mathrm{B} = i_\mathrm{C}/\beta$, with $\beta$ somewhere between 50 and 500 and not to be relied on. Design so that $\beta$ does not appear in the answer; it varies between parts, with current and with temperature.

Three regions:

  • Cutoff. $v_\mathrm{BE}$ below about 0.5 V, no current.
  • Forward active. $v_\mathrm{BE}\approx 0.6$–$0.7$ V, collector-base reverse biased. $i_\mathrm{C}$ set by $v_\mathrm{BE}$ and nearly independent of $v_\mathrm{CE}$. This is the useful region for amplifiers.
  • Saturation. $v_\mathrm{CE}$ below about 0.2 V, both junctions forward biased, $i_\mathrm{C}$ now set by the external circuit. This is the useful region for switches.

In the active region the curves are not quite flat. Extrapolated backwards they meet the voltage axis at $-V_\mathrm{A}$, the Early voltage, 50 to 150 V, and that slope is a finite output resistance

$$r_\mathrm{o} = \frac{V_\mathrm{A}}{I_\mathrm{C}} .$$

Say that again in chapter 1's language: a transistor in the active region is a real current source whose internal resistance is $V_\mathrm{A}/I_\mathrm{C}$. At 1 mA with $V_\mathrm{A} = 100\ \mathrm{V}$ that is 100 kΩ. The question chapter 1 left open has an answer and the answer is a part.

The MOSFET

Above the threshold voltage $V_\mathrm{th}$ and in saturation, the drain current is approximately a square law,

$$\begin{aligned} i_\mathrm{D} &= \frac{k}{2}\,(v_\mathrm{GS} - V_\mathrm{th})^2\,(1 + \lambda v_\mathrm{DS}) ,\\[4pt] k &= \mu C_\mathrm{ox}\frac{W}{L} , \end{aligned}$$

where $W/L$ is the width-to-length ratio the designer chooses, $\mu C_\mathrm{ox}$ is a process constant and $\lambda$ plays the role of $1/V_\mathrm{A}$. The quantity $v_\mathrm{GS} - V_\mathrm{th}$ is the overdrive, and most design decisions are really a choice of overdrive.

Below the threshold the device does not switch off abruptly; it goes exponential, with roughly 60–90 mV per decade, which is the same physics as the BJT. Low-power analog design deliberately works there.

Two differences from the BJT that decide which to use:

  • The gate draws no DC current. No base current, no $\beta$, no bias current error of the kind chapter 6 listed. It is a capacitor, so it costs charge at high frequency instead.
  • $g_\mathrm{m}$ per unit current is worse. For the BJT $g_\mathrm{m} = I_\mathrm{C}/V_\mathrm{T}$, fixed by physics; for the MOSFET $g_\mathrm{m} = 2I_\mathrm{D}/(v_\mathrm{GS}-V_\mathrm{th})$, and the overdrive is typically 200 mV against the BJT's 26 mV. A bipolar input stage therefore gives more gain and lower noise for the same current, which is why precision op-amps still use them.

Bias: choosing where to sit

An amplifier must sit somewhere on the curve before it can amplify. Choosing that point is biasing, and it is a large-signal DC problem solved with chapter 1's load line, now drawn across a family of curves instead of one.

The naive approach, driving the base from a fixed voltage, does not survive contact with reality: $i_\mathrm{C}$ depends exponentially on $v_\mathrm{BE}$, and $v_\mathrm{BE}$ shifts by $-2\ \mathrm{mV/K}$ (chapter 5). A 10 K rise multiplies the current by more than two, and thermal runaway is a real failure mode.

The fix is local negative feedback: put a resistor $R_\mathrm{E}$ in the emitter. The emitter current then sets its own voltage, which opposes any increase, and

$$I_\mathrm{C} \approx \frac{V_\mathrm{B} - V_\mathrm{BE}}{R_\mathrm{E}} .$$

Neither $\beta$ nor $I_\mathrm{S}$ appears. The exponential has been traded for a division by a resistor, and the price is some of the gain. This is chapter 6's trade — accuracy bought with gain — applied one level further down, and it is why degeneration appears in almost every discrete stage.

Small signal

Same move as chapter 5. Fix the operating point, then linearize. The transconductance is the slope of the control curve,

$$g_\mathrm{m} = \frac{\partial i_\mathrm{C}}{\partial v_\mathrm{BE}} = \frac{I_\mathrm{C}}{V_\mathrm{T}} ,$$

38.6 mS at 1 mA, and the input resistance looking into the base is $r_\pi = \beta/g_\mathrm{m}$. With the output resistance $r_\mathrm{o}$ from the Early effect, these three elements are the hybrid-$\pi$ model, and the whole of chapters 1 to 4 then applies to the small signal.

The intrinsic gain of the device, the most it can give in one stage, is

$$g_\mathrm{m}r_\mathrm{o} = \frac{I_\mathrm{C}}{V_\mathrm{T}}\cdot\frac{V_\mathrm{A}}{I_\mathrm{C}} = \frac{V_\mathrm{A}}{V_\mathrm{T}} ,$$

about 4000 for $V_\mathrm{A} = 100\ \mathrm{V}$, and independent of the current. That number is why an op-amp needs only two gain stages to reach $10^5$.

Three ways to wire one transistor

Which terminal is common to input and output names the stage, and the three have genuinely different jobs.

common emitter common collector (follower) common base
gain $-g_\mathrm{m}R_\mathrm{C}$, large, inverting $\approx 1$ $+g_\mathrm{m}R_\mathrm{C}$, large
input resistance $r_\pi$, moderate high, $\beta R_\mathrm{E}$ low, $1/g_\mathrm{m}$
output resistance high, $R_\mathrm{C}$ low, $\approx 1/g_\mathrm{m}$ high
job voltage gain buffer current buffer, wide band

The emitter follower deserves the same attention the op-amp buffer got in chapter 6, because it is the same function with one component: high input resistance, low output resistance, unity gain. Before op-amps existed, this is how a weak node was made strong, and it is still how an op-amp's own output stage is built.

The common base stage has no Miller effect — the input and output are not bridged by the collector-base capacitance — so it keeps working where the common emitter has run out of bandwidth. A common emitter followed by a common base is the cascode, which gets the gain of the first and the bandwidth of the second.

The differential pair

Two matched transistors, emitters tied together, fed by a tail current source $I_\mathrm{T}$. The tail splits according to the input difference, and for the bipolar version the split is exactly

$$i_{\mathrm{C}1} - i_{\mathrm{C}2} = I_\mathrm{T}\tanh\!\left(\frac{v_\mathrm{id}}{2V_\mathrm{T}}\right) ,$$

with $v_\mathrm{id}$ the differential input. Three readings of this one line:

  • Near zero it is linear, with a slope $g_\mathrm{m} = I_\mathrm{T}/(2V_\mathrm{T})$. That is the input stage of every op-amp in chapter 6, and the linear range is a few $V_\mathrm{T}$: tens of millivolts, which is exactly why an op-amp needs feedback to be usable.
  • It saturates gracefully. Beyond about $4V_\mathrm{T}$ all the tail goes one way. The $\tanh$ is a soft limiter, and that is useful on purpose in oscillators, where something has to set the amplitude.
  • It rejects what is common. Move both inputs together and the tail current does not care, so the output does not move. The common-mode rejection is set by how constant the tail source is, that is by its output resistance, which is chapter 1 again.

The same pair with the tail current itself modulated by a second input is the Gilbert cell, a four-quadrant multiplier. It is the standard analog multiplier and the standard mixer, and it works because $\tanh$ is odd and the tail scales the whole characteristic. The multiplier that chapter 7 listed as an analog-computer motif is this circuit.

The current mirror

Two matched transistors with their bases tied and one of them diode-connected. The diode-connected one converts an input current into the $v_\mathrm{BE}$ that produces it; the other, seeing the same $v_\mathrm{BE}$, produces the same current:

$$I_\mathrm{out} = I_\mathrm{in}\,\frac{A_2}{A_1} ,$$

scaled by the ratio of device areas. The output resistance is $r_\mathrm{o}$, so a mirror is a good current source in chapter 1's sense, and the accuracy depends on matching rather than on absolute values. Integrated analog design is built on this observation: absolute component values on a chip are poor, 20 % or worse, while ratios between adjacent identical devices are excellent, a fraction of a percent. Every bias current on a chip is a mirrored copy of one reference, and the reference is the bandgap built from chapter 5's $\Delta v = V_\mathrm{T}\ln(i_1/i_2)$.

Inside the op-amp

Chapter 6 treated the device as a black box. It is four blocks, all now familiar.

  1. A differential pair as the input stage, setting the offset, the noise and the input current.
  2. A current mirror as its load, which converts the differential current into a single-ended one and gives the stage a high impedance and therefore a large gain.
  3. A second gain stage, a common emitter with a compensation capacitor across it. That capacitor is what creates the dominant pole of chapter 6, and the gain-bandwidth product is $g_\mathrm{m1}/(2\pi C_\mathrm{c})$. The slew rate is $I_\mathrm{T}/C_\mathrm{c}$: the tail current charging the same capacitor. Two datasheet numbers, one capacitor.
  4. An output stage, a complementary pair of followers, giving current drive at unity voltage gain, with the output current limit and the crossover distortion that come with it.

Chapter 6's list of imperfections was not arbitrary. Each entry on it is one of these four blocks failing to be ideal, and knowing which block is responsible is how you pick a part.

The transistor as a switch

Drive the base or gate hard and the device leaves the active region. A bipolar in saturation drops $V_\mathrm{CE(sat)} \approx 0.2\ \mathrm{V}$, so it dissipates $0.2\ \mathrm{V}\times I$ regardless of how small $I$ is. A MOSFET in triode behaves as a resistance $R_\mathrm{DS(on)}$, milliohms in a power part, so it dissipates $I^2R$ and gets better as the current falls. That single difference is why power switching is MOSFETs and why saturated bipolar logic was abandoned.

Digital electronics is this and nothing else: transistors driven to the ends of their characteristics, where the only two interesting numbers are the on resistance and the charge needed to get there. The whole of chapters 1 to 7 is about the region in between, which digital design works hard to pass through quickly and which analog design has to live in.

Exercises

  1. A common emitter stage runs at $I_\mathrm{C} = 1\ \mathrm{mA}$ with $R_\mathrm{C} = 4.7\ \mathrm{k}\Omega$. Find $g_\mathrm{m}$, the voltage gain, and the gain once a 100 Ω emitter resistor is added. (Roughly $-181$, falling to $-45$: the degeneration costs a factor of four and buys bias stability.)
  2. Show that adding $R_\mathrm{E}$ changes the gain to $-R_\mathrm{C}/(1/g_\mathrm{m} + R_\mathrm{E})$, and explain why this form makes the stage's linearity much better.
  3. A differential pair has $I_\mathrm{T} = 100\ \mathrm{\mu A}$. Over what differential input is the $\tanh$ within 1 % of its tangent at the origin? Compare with the input offset voltage of a typical op-amp.
  4. On the transistor bench, pick an operating point in the middle of the load line and read the gain. Then move the point close to each end and read it again. Explain both changes, and say which end clips first.
