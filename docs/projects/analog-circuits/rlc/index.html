Inductors, RLC and resonance

A second energy store turns the exponential into a ringing. The second-order equation, the three damping regimes and the pole pair behind them, resonance and the quality factor, and what happens to a circuit when the damping is driven towards zero. This is the chapter the oscillator work stands on.

Chapter 4 of 8 · 22 November 2026

The other way to store energy

A capacitor stores energy in an electric field and resists a change of voltage. An inductor stores energy in a magnetic field and resists a change of current. Everything else follows by swapping the words.

$$\begin{aligned} v &= L\,\frac{\mathrm{d}i}{\mathrm{d}t} ,& W &= \tfrac{1}{2}L\,i^2 ,& Z_L &= \mathrm{i}\omega L , \end{aligned}$$

where $L$ is the inductance in henries, $v$ the voltage across the coil and $i$ the current through it. The impedance follows from the same substitution as before: put $i = \underline{I}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega t}$ into the defining relation and the derivative becomes a factor $\mathrm{i}\omega$.

The duality is exact, and writing it out once saves re-deriving anything:

capacitor inductor
state variable voltage $v$ current $i$
defining relation $i = C\,\dot v$ $v = L\,\dot i$
cannot jump the voltage the current
energy $\tfrac12 Cv^2$ $\tfrac12 Li^2$
impedance $1/(\mathrm{i}\omega C)$ $\mathrm{i}\omega L$
at DC open circuit short circuit
at high frequency short circuit open circuit
phase current leads by $90^\circ$ current lags by $90^\circ$

The "cannot jump" row deserves the same warning chapter 1 gave about sources. Interrupting the current in an inductor is the dual of shorting a capacitor: the device tries to produce whatever voltage it takes to keep the current going, and in a mechanical switch that voltage arrives as a spark. This is why every relay coil in every circuit has a diode across it, and chapter 5 explains what that diode does.

Two stores, one equation

Put $L$, $R$ and $C$ in series across a source $v_\mathrm{in}$. Kirchhoff's voltage law and the three component relations give one equation in the capacitor voltage,

$$LC\,\ddot v_C + RC\,\dot v_C + v_C = v_\mathrm{in} .$$

This is a damped harmonic oscillator, which is the same equation as a mass on a spring in a viscous fluid, and the correspondence is worth keeping: $L$ is the mass, $1/C$ the spring constant, $R$ the drag. Put it in the standard form

$$\ddot v_C + 2\zeta\omega_0\,\dot v_C + \omega_0^2\,v_C = \omega_0^2\,v_\mathrm{in} ,$$

with the two parameters that replace the three components,

$$\begin{aligned} \omega_0 &= \frac{1}{\sqrt{LC}} ,\\[4pt] \zeta &= \frac{R}{2}\sqrt{\frac{C}{L}} ,\\[4pt] Q &= \frac{1}{2\zeta} = \frac{1}{R}\sqrt{\frac{L}{C}} . \end{aligned}$$

Here $\omega_0$ is the undamped natural frequency, $\zeta$ the damping ratio and $Q$ the quality factor. Three components collapse into two numbers, and only those two numbers matter for the shape of the response. $L$ and $C$ set where it happens, $R$ sets how violently.

The characteristic roots are

$$s_{1,2} = -\zeta\omega_0 \pm \omega_0\sqrt{\zeta^2 - 1} ,$$

and everything the circuit does in time is read off their position in the complex plane. The real part is the decay rate; the imaginary part, when there is one, is the ringing frequency.

Three regimes

$\zeta$ roots step response
$\zeta > 1$ overdamped two real, negative two exponentials, no overshoot, slow
$\zeta = 1$ critically damped one double real root fastest approach without overshoot
$\zeta < 1$ underdamped complex conjugate pair ringing at $\omega_\mathrm{d}$, decaying as $\mathrm{e}^{-\zeta\omega_0 t}$

For the underdamped case the damped frequency is

$$\omega_\mathrm{d} = \omega_0\sqrt{1 - \zeta^2} ,$$

and the step response of the capacitor voltage is

$$v_C(t) = V\left[1 - \mathrm{e}^{-\zeta\omega_0 t} \left(\cos\omega_\mathrm{d}t + \frac{\zeta}{\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}}\sin\omega_\mathrm{d}t\right)\right] .$$

The first overshoot is a clean function of the damping alone,

$$\frac{v_\mathrm{peak} - V}{V} = \exp\!\left(\frac{-\pi\zeta}{\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}}\right) ,$$

which gives 16 % at $\zeta = 0.5$, 4.3 % at $\zeta = 0.707$ and 0.0 % at $\zeta = 1$. The value $\zeta = 1/\sqrt{2}$ turns up constantly because it is the damping that makes the frequency response as flat as possible without peaking. It is called the Butterworth response and chapter 7 builds filters out of it.

Critical damping is the fastest settling, not the fastest rise. An overdamped circuit rises more slowly; an underdamped one rises faster but then spends time ringing. If the specification is "within 1 % and stay there", $\zeta$ slightly below 1 usually wins.

The bench

Drag $R$ down from large to small and watch the two real roots approach each other on the negative real axis, collide at $\zeta = 1$, and then split into a conjugate pair that walks towards the imaginary axis. The step response does the corresponding thing. The pole picture and the time picture are the same statement.

Resonance

Drive the same series circuit with a sinusoid instead of a step. The total impedance is

$$Z(\omega) = R + \mathrm{i}\omega L + \frac{1}{\mathrm{i}\omega C} = R + \mathrm{i}\left(\omega L - \frac{1}{\omega C}\right) .$$

The reactive part vanishes when $\omega L = 1/(\omega C)$, that is at $\omega = \omega_0$. There the impedance is purely resistive and at its smallest, the current is at its largest, and the circuit is in resonance. The voltages across $L$ and $C$ individually are then $Q$ times the drive and cancel each other exactly. A series RLC with $Q = 50$ driven by 1 V has 50 V across the inductor, which surprises people once.

Reading the output across the resistor gives a band-pass,

$$H(\omega) = \frac{R}{Z(\omega)} = \frac{1}{1 + \mathrm{i}Q\left(\dfrac{\omega}{\omega_0} - \dfrac{\omega_0}{\omega}\right)} ,$$

whose magnitude is 1 at $\omega_0$ and falls on both sides. The half-power points are where the bracket equals $\pm 1/Q$, and the width between them is

$$\Delta\omega = \frac{\omega_0}{Q} = \frac{R}{L} .$$

$Q$ is the ratio of centre frequency to bandwidth, which is the definition to remember, and it is numerically the same as

$$Q = 2\pi\,\frac{\text{energy stored}}{\text{energy lost per cycle}} ,$$

which is the definition that explains why it is called a quality factor and which transfers unchanged to a pendulum, an optical cavity or a nuclear resonance. A circuit and an atom are described by the same two numbers here.

What happens as the damping goes to zero

Follow the pole pair as $R$ falls. It approaches the imaginary axis, the ringing lasts longer, the resonance gets taller and narrower. At $R = 0$ the poles sit exactly on the axis and the circuit rings forever at $\omega_0$, exchanging energy between the two stores without loss. That is an oscillator, and it is also an idealization no more realistic than the ideal sources of chapter 1.

Every real coil and every real capacitor has loss, so the poles sit in the left half plane and any disturbance dies. To build an oscillator you have to put the energy back, with a circuit that behaves like a negative resistance over the amplitude range of interest. Then the poles cross into the right half plane, the amplitude grows, and some nonlinearity has to catch it and hold it. An oscillator is therefore never a linear circuit, however linear its small-signal model looks: amplitude is set by the nonlinearity and frequency by the resonance.

This is where the course connects to what I do for a living. A phase-locked loop is a resonator of this kind wrapped in a feedback loop that steers $\omega_0$, and a network of them is what I write down as coupled phase equations. In the models the quantity that matters is the phase; $Q$ enters as the bandwidth over which a phase disturbance survives, and $R$ only appears disguised as a damping constant. Here it is a part number.

Real inductors are the worst component

A capacitor is close to ideal over a wide range. An inductor is not, and the differences show up early:

  • Series resistance. The wire has resistance, and it is not small. A 10 mH coil might carry 20 Ω, which already caps $Q$ before anything else does.
  • Self-resonance. The turns have capacitance between them. Above its self-resonant frequency an inductor is a capacitor, and the datasheet gives the frequency where it stops being what it says.
  • Core saturation. Any inductor with a magnetic core has a current above which the permeability collapses and the inductance drops. The circuit then changes behaviour as a function of signal amplitude.
  • It is physically large and it couples. An inductor broadcasts and receives a magnetic field, so two of them near each other are a transformer whether or not that was the intention.

For these reasons integrated analog design avoids inductors below the hundreds of megahertz and synthesizes the behaviour instead. A gyrator is a circuit that makes a capacitor look inductive from one port, and chapter 7 builds resonant responses out of op-amps, resistors and capacitors with no coil anywhere. The second-order equation of this chapter is what those circuits reproduce; the component that produced it is optional.

Exercises

  1. A series RLC has $L = 10\ \mathrm{mH}$, $C = 100\ \mathrm{nF}$ and $R = 50\ \Omega$. Find $f_0$, $\zeta$, $Q$, the bandwidth and the first overshoot of the step response. (Answers: 5.03 kHz, 0.079, 6.32, 796 Hz, 78 %.)
  2. Show that the parallel RLC is the dual of the series one, with $Q = R\sqrt{C/L}$. Note that $R$ now has to be large for a high $Q$, which is the same reversal as between the two sources in chapter 1.
  3. At resonance the voltage across the inductor is $Q$ times the drive. Where does the extra energy come from, and why does this not violate anything?
  4. Set $\zeta = 1/\sqrt{2}$ on bench 7 and read the overshoot; then set the same $\zeta$ on bench 8 and confirm the magnitude response has no peak. Those two facts are the same fact.
