Strahlenoptik: a small optics lab

Five stations of ray optics to take hold of: light and shadow, the law of reflection, refraction and total reflection, the focal point of a lens, and an optical bench with a light source, an object, a lens on a rail and a projection screen. Every station carries a short explanation and tasks that check themselves. German, English, Spanish and French.

Why this exists

Optics is the first piece of physics a child can check by hand. A shadow is either there or it is not. A ray either hits the target or misses it. The image on the screen is either sharp or it is blurred, and nobody has to be told which. That makes it the rare topic where a simulation is not a substitute for the experiment but a rehearsal for it: everything in here can be rebuilt on a kitchen table with a torch, a mirror, a glass of water and a magnifying glass, and it should be.

What a screen adds is the part the kitchen table hides. On a real optical bench you see the image and not the rays. Here the three rays that construct the image are drawn while you move the lens, so the rule and its consequence move together. That is the whole argument for doing it this way, and it is also the limit: a drawn ray is a claim about geometry, not a photograph.

So geht es

Für die Kinder, auf Deutsch. Oben wählst du die Station, rechts oben die Sprache: DE · EN · ES · FR. Jede Station erklärt sich in drei, vier Sätzen, dann darfst du ziehen. Die dicken orangenen Punkte im Bild fasst du mit der Maus oder mit dem Finger an; die Regler darunter tun genau dasselbe und gehen auch mit der Tastatur. Unter dem Bild stehen die Aufgaben. Die prüfen sich selbst: sobald es stimmt, hakt sich die Aufgabe ab und bleibt abgehakt, auch wenn du die Seite später wieder aufmachst.

Die fünf Stationen bauen aufeinander auf. Licht & Schatten zeigt, dass Licht geradeaus läuft, und mit der zweiten Lampe, woher Kernschatten und Halbschatten kommen. Spiegel ist die eine Regel, die man wirklich braucht: Einfallswinkel gleich Ausfallswinkel, gemessen zum Lot. Brechung zeigt, warum ein Stab im Wasser geknickt aussieht, und den Grenzwinkel, ab dem gar kein Licht mehr herauskommt. Brennpunkt sammelt paralleles Licht in einem Punkt. Und im optischen Labor steht dann alles zusammen auf der Linsenschiene: Lampe, Gegenstand, Linse, Projektionswand.

What is in it

Station What you move What it is for
Licht & Schatten lamp, object, wall, a second lamp light travels straight; umbra and penumbra
Spiegel where the ray hits, how the mirror is turned the law of reflection, and the image behind the glass
Brechung the angle, water or glass, which side the ray comes from Snell's law, and total reflection
Brennpunkt focal length, tilt of the bundle, lens type the focal point and the focal plane
Optisches Labor object, lens, screen, focal length, object size the thin-lens equation and the image it predicts

Eighteen tasks across the five, and the ticks live in the browser. There is no account, no timer and no score beyond how many of the eighteen have been solved once.

How it is built

The lab is one file of JavaScript with no dependencies and no network traffic. Each station holds its model in centimetres and degrees, and the drawing maps centimetres to pixels at the last moment. That split is worth naming because it is what makes the thing checkable: the physics never learns that a screen exists, so it can be run without one.

That is exactly what tools/optics_proof.js does. It loads the applet against a stub window, pulls the stations out through a test hook and sweeps the whole control space of every station. It answers two questions that looking at the screen cannot:

  • Is every task reachable? A task whose window the sliders cannot enter is not a hard task, it is a broken one. Three of the eighteen sit in under a tenth of a percent of the swept space, which is the point of them, and none sits at zero.
  • Is any task satisfied nearly everywhere? Then it teaches nothing. One was: push the object inside the focal length is a single shove of a slider. It now asks for a magnification of two as well, which forces the object close to the focal point — which is where a magnifier is actually held. The check caught a weak task, not a wrong one, and that is the more common failure.

The same script checks the physics against values worked out by hand: air into water at 40° refracts to 28.9°, the critical angle out of water is 48.75° and out of glass 41.8°, an object at twice the focal length gives an image at twice the focal length the same size as itself, and a mirror returns a ray at the angle it arrived at for every tilt of the mirror. It also checks that every station, control, readout and task carries text in all four languages, because a missing string is the kind of fault that only shows up in the one language nobody tested.

A few decisions that are visible in the pictures:

  • The shadow station draws rays, not a shaded cone. The shadow is defined as the part of the wall no ray reaches, so drawing the rays and letting the gap appear is the definition rather than an illustration of it.
  • The half-shadow here is made by two lamps, not by one large lamp. A real penumbra is the smooth result of an extended source, and a lamp with a size would be the honest model. Two point lamps give two shadows with a region where only one of them falls, which is the same idea in two steps instead of a continuum. It is easier to see and it is not the same thing.
  • The lens obeys the thin-lens equation and nothing else. $1/f = 1/g + 1/b$, with the three principal rays drawn as the construction. That is the paraxial approximation: no thickness, no spherical aberration, no colour. A real lens of this aperture would not bring the edge rays to the same point.
  • The spot on the screen is blurred in proportion to the defocus. The blur radius is the aperture the bundle uses times the defocus divided by the image distance, which is the geometry of a cone cut off the wrong side of its tip. It is there so that sharp is something you hunt for rather than something the applet announces.
  • The zoom-free viewBox is set to the element's pixel size. Strokes and labels are therefore in pixels and keep their size on a phone, while the model stays in centimetres. The blind map solved the same problem the other way round, with a CSS variable, because that one has to zoom.

What it does not do

It is ray optics and only ray optics. Light is a straight line that bends at boundaries and nothing else, which is a model that stops being true as soon as anything gets small: there is no diffraction here, no interference, no polarization, no wavelength and therefore no colour. A prism in this lab would bend white light and not split it.

The mirror is flat. A concave mirror has a focal point too, and it is the other half of the schoolbook chapter, so its absence is the most obvious gap. The eye and the camera are named in one task each and are not modelled. There is no lens combination, which is what a microscope and a telescope are.

And it checks arithmetic, not understanding. A child can solve every task by sliding until the tick appears. Whether the picture went in is a question only a person can ask, which is the same honest limit the blind map has.
